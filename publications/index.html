Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin

The Morality-Effectiveness Disconnect of Pro-Environmental Behaviors

Jareef Martuza & Hege Landsvik

People judge the climate actions that help most, such as flying less, as less moral than actions that help little, such as recycling.

Five main and six supplemental studies (N = 9,247 adults) across five countries (the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and South Africa) reveal a robust morality-effectiveness disconnect: pro-environmental behaviors that rank better on objective climate-mitigation potential rank worse on individual moral judgments. Laypeople fail to predict this pattern. Detailed impact information reverses the disconnect and increases policy endorsement of more effective behaviors.

Journal of Experimental Social Psychology

Revisiting Gino, Ayal, et al.’s (2009) contagion and differentiation in unethical behavior: A registered conceptual replication and extension

Jareef Martuza & Esra Aslan

Seeing a member of one’s own group cheat did not lead to more cheating than seeing an outsider cheat.

Gino, Ayal, et al. (2009) found that individuals behave more dishonestly after witnessing dishonesty by an ingroup member than by an outgroup member. In a registered conceptual replication and extension (N = 3,323 adults) using both minimal and natural groups, the preregistered ingroup-outgroup hypotheses were not supported, and Bayesian analyses provided moderate to strong evidence for null effects.

Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin

Does Self-Projection Have a Dominant Temporal Direction? In Nine Countries, People Feel More Similar to Their Future Than Past Selves

Jareef Martuza, Simen Bø, Helge Thorbjørnsen & Hallgeir Sjåstad

People feel more similar to their future selves than to their past selves, in all nine countries studied.

Compared to the present, 12 studies (N = 11,416 adults) reveal that people feel more similar to their future selves than their equidistant past selves, consistently across nine countries (the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Germany, Italy, Poland, Portugal, and South Africa). Future-oriented self-projection can lead to miscalibrated expectations: people expect their future selves to be more satisfied with decisions made in the present than they currently would be with the same decisions in the past.

Journal of Experimental Social Psychology

Beliefs versus reality: People overestimate the actual dishonesty of others

Jareef Martuza, Helge Thorbjørnsen & Hallgeir Sjåstad

People overestimate how many others would lie by 13.6 percentage points.

An internal meta-analysis of 31 effects from 11 experiments (N = 8,126 adults) revealed that people overestimate others’ dishonest behavior by 13.6 percentage points on average. Professional managers also hold pessimistic beliefs about real-world dishonesty, and this moral pessimism predicts greater support for freedom-restrictive countermeasures such as surveillance.

Human Is Gold: Why Premium Customers Hate Chatbots and What to Do About It

Aruna Divya Tatavarthy, Jareef Martuza & Helge Thorbjørnsen

Premium customers rate the same chatbot service worse than basic customers do.

High-tier (vs. basic) customers react more negatively to chatbot-delivered services, even when the objective service delivered is the same. Three preregistered experiments (N = 3,114 adults) point to uniqueness neglect and entitlement as explanations, and show three framing interventions that reduce this bias.

The role of social norms in retirement saving: Evidence from two natural field experiments

Trond Døskeland, Jareef Martuza, Lars Jacob Tynes Pedersen, Francisco Santos, Hallgeir Sjåstad & Helge Thorbjørnsen

Telling people what most others do raised retirement saving more than telling them what experts advise.

Two large-scale field experiments with Norwegian bank customers (N = 455,509 and 224,000 adults) tested the effects of different norm messages on actual saving behavior. Information about what most other people do (descriptive norms) was more effective in increasing retirement savings than expert opinion on what one should do (injunctive norms).

The Journal of Positive Psychology

Not too little and not too much? The relationships between spare time, spare money, and subjective well-being

Jareef Martuza, Micael Dahlen & Helge Thorbjørnsen

More spare money raises well-being up to a point, while a lot of spare time lowers it.

Surveys in the USA, the UK, and Germany (N = 1,288 adults) show that the relationship between spare money and well-being is positive until a certain point, after which it becomes non-significant. The relationship between spare time and well-being has an inverted U shape: well-being increases with spare time at low levels but decreases at high levels.

Effectiveness of ex ante honesty oaths in reducing dishonesty depends on content

Janis H. Zickfeld et al., including Jareef Martuza

The best of 21 honesty oaths nearly halved tax evasion, but neither experts nor laypeople could predict which oaths would work best.

In a megastudy (N = 21,506 adults), we tested 21 honesty oaths in a tax-evasion game. The best oath nearly halved tax evasion, but neither experts nor laypeople could predict which oaths would work best.

Social Psychological and Personality Science

A Registered Report on Gender Bias in Interpersonal Dishonesty: Are Females and Males Cheated Differently?

Jareef Martuza

Women cheated other women 53.6% less than they cheated men.

Participants from nine countries (Australia, Canada, Germany, Italy, Poland, Portugal, South Africa, the United Kingdom, and the United States; N = 3,166 adults) could lie to increase their payoffs at the cost of another same-sex, opposite-sex, or unmentioned-sex participant, with zero risk of detection. Female decision-makers cheated female (vs. male) targets 53.6% less, while male decision-makers cheated male and female targets similarly.

Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin

Does Conceptual Abstraction Moderate Whether Past Moral Deeds Motivate Consistency or Compensatory Behavior? A Registered Replication and Extension of Conway and Peetz (2012)

Jareef Martuza & Olivia Kim

Recalling a past good deed made people more willing to help others, whether the deed was recent or long ago.

Conway and Peetz (2012) proposed that conceptual abstraction moderates whether past moral deeds lead to consistent or compensatory behavior. In a registered report (N = 5,091 adults), the hypothesized interaction was not replicated: recalling moral behavior led to higher prosocial intentions whether it was recalled from the recent or distant past. We found no evidence for compensatory moral behavior, only for positive moral consistency.

Do honesty-nudges really work? A large-scale field experiment in an insurance context

Jareef Bin Martuza, Siv Rosendahl Skard, Lavrans Løvlie & Helge Thorbjørnsen

None of three honesty nudges reduced signs of fraud in 5,704 real insurance claims.

In a controlled field experiment with customers filing real insurance claims online (N = 5,704 adults), we tested three honesty-nudges: signing at the beginning, a descriptive social norm message, and a solidarity message. None of the nudges, standalone or combined, reduced signs of fraud.
